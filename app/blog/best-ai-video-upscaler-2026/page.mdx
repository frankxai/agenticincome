## What upscaling actually does

AI upscaling uses a trained model to intelligently add pixels to an image or video, increasing resolution while trying to preserve (or sharpen) detail, rather than just stretching and blurring the original — which is what non-AI resizing does. It can also reduce noise, fix compression artifacts, sharpen faces, and stabilize shaky footage, depending on the tool.

What it cannot do: invent detail that was never in the source. If a face was a 20-pixel blur in the original clip, upscaling will produce a *sharper-looking* 20-pixel blur — not a photorealistic face. Upscaling improves and enlarges existing information. It doesn't hallucinate new information convincingly enough to fool close inspection.

## Who actually needs this

- **Old footage restoration** — home videos, camcorder tapes, early digital-camera footage, or archival material shot well below HD. This is the clearest, most defensible use case.
- **Low-res source material** — stock footage, old project files, or client-supplied assets that were never captured at the resolution you need for the final deliverable.
- **AI-video-gen output cleanup** — most AI video generators (Higgsfield, Kling, Veo, Sora, and others) output at resolutions that look fine on a phone screen but fall apart on a large display or in a professional deliverable. Upscaling is the standard second step after generation, not a sign something went wrong.
- **Print or large-format from digital photos** — a photo that looks sharp on a screen can look soft blown up to poster size. Photo upscaling closes that gap.

## Who should skip it

If your footage was shot on a modern camera at 4K or higher and you're delivering at 1080p or 4K, you don't need this — you already have more resolution than you're using. Buying an upscaler here solves a problem you don't have. The same goes for images shot on a recent phone or camera at high megapixel counts for standard web or social use. Save the money.
